Read this page before you change light submission, light culling, or the
lighting terms of material evaluation. The units and
limits an application sees are specified in the
[lighting reference](/docs/reference/lighting).

## Units are resolved on the CPU

Byte Engine resolves authoring units on the CPU. The GPU receives RGB lux for
directional lights and RGB candela for point and cone lights. Local lights use
inverse-square attenuation, and the physically based (PBR) material evaluation
applies the diffuse and specular bidirectional reflectance distribution function
(BRDF) terms that [diffuse takes what specular leaves](#diffuse-takes-what-specular-leaves)
describes.

For a local light, the incident RGB quantity at a surface is
`candela / distance²`, multiplied by the cone factor when applicable. For a
directional light, the incident RGB quantity is its lux value directly. Shadows
and material BRDF terms then reduce or redistribute that incident light. They
don't change the authored unit conversion.

The PBR visibility pipeline and the atmosphere sky apply the camera exposure as
they write scene light, so later passes such as bloom and tone mapping receive
exposed light. Scenes drawn with other pipelines ignore exposure.

## Diffuse takes what specular leaves

Light that reaches a surface either reflects off its specular lobe or enters
the surface and scatters out as diffuse light. The specular lobe's directional
albedo `E(μ)` is the share of light arriving at cosine `μ` that it reflects. The
diffuse term takes the rest of the light on the way in and on the way out, after
Kelemen and Szirmay-Kalos (A Microfacet Based Coupled Specular-Matte BRDF Model
with Importance Sampling, 2001):

`f_d = (1 − metalness) · albedo / π · (1 − E(μl)) · (1 − E(μv)) / (1 − E_avg)`

- `μl` and `μv` are the cosines between the shading normal and the light and
  view directions.
- `E(μ) = F0 · A + B`, where `A` and `B` come from Karis's fit of the split-sum
  environment BRDF, the same one that weighs reflected light. The diffuse part
  belongs to the dielectric layer, so it uses the dielectric `F0` of 0.04.
- `E_avg` is the cosine-weighted average of `E(μ)` over the hemisphere. A
  quadratic in roughness fits it within 0.004, so shaders don't integrate it.
  Dividing by `1 − E_avg` keeps a surface lit from every direction as bright as
  `(1 − E(μv)) · albedo`.

Indirect diffuse light arrives from the whole hemisphere, so its weight is that
average: `(1 − metalness) · (1 − E(μv)) · albedo`.

A rough surface's lobe reflects little light at any angle, so its diffuse light
stays close to Lambert's even at a grazing view. A smooth surface's lobe reflects
nearly everything at grazing angles, so its diffuse light fades there. A Fresnel
factor of the macroscopic normal alone, `1 − F(μ)`, assumes a mirror-smooth
surface and darkens rough surfaces seen edge-on.

A change here must keep these rules:

- Diffuse and specular together never reflect more than the light that arrives:
  for any view, `E(μv)` plus the diffuse albedo over every light direction stays
  at or below one.
- Diffuse light stays above zero at a grazing view on a rough surface.

## Thin surfaces pass light to their far side

A leaf is a thin sheet: part of the light that enters one face scatters out of
the other. A material says how much with the BEMX `translucency` input, and
the [rendering reference](/docs/reference/rendering#translucent-materials)
specifies what it adds. This isn't subsurface scattering in the sense of light
spreading sideways under skin: nothing is blurred across pixels, and the pixel
only gains light from lights behind it.

The light loop of `MATERIAL_EVALUATION_SUFFIX_SOURCE` drops a light whose
cosine with the shading normal isn't positive. For a translucent material it
keeps a light with a negative cosine, takes the cosine on the far side, runs
the same attenuation and shadow code, and then adds diffuse light only:

`f_t = (1 − metalness) · translucency / π · (1 − E(μl)) · (1 − E(μv)) / (1 − E_avg)`

That's the coupled diffuse term with `translucency` for the albedo and `μl`
measured on the far side, so the specular lobe takes its share on the way in
and on the way out exactly as it does for near-side light.

The far-side code reads `translucency` or a local computed from it, and
`material_evaluation_suffix_statements` in `shader_generator/ast.rs` removes
every such statement from a material that doesn't set the input. In the uber
mode, translucent materials have
[shaders of their own](/docs/develop/rendering/visibility/material-evaluation#translucent-materials-take-their-own-shaders).

A light behind a leaf must still reach it through the shadow map. Both sun
shadow sites, `resolve_sun_shadow` in `sun-visibility.besl` and the transparent
path of the light loop, move the receiver off its triangle on the sun's side.
On the camera's side, a back-lit leaf would move behind its own depth in the
map and shadow itself.

A change here must keep these rules:

- **Pay only where it's used.** Every far-side statement reads `translucency`
  or a local computed from it, so a material without the input compiles none
  of them.
- **Share the light's path.** Far-side light uses the loop's attenuation,
  cone, IES, and shadow code. Don't copy them into a second loop.
- **Leave the receiver on the light's side.** A normal offset moves the
  receiver toward the light it's tested against, never toward the camera.
- **Keep the energy rule.** With `base_color` plus `translucency` at or under
  one, near-side and far-side diffuse light plus specular light never exceed
  the light that arrives.

## Lights are culled per cluster

Every frame, the renderer splits each camera's view into a grid of clusters on
the GPU and records which lights reach each cluster. Each pixel is then shaded
only with the lights that can reach it, so a scene with many small lights costs
about as much as the few lights near each surface. A point or cone light
reaches as far as its exposed illuminance stays above 1/1024 lux, so reach
grows with the light's intensity and the camera exposure. The light list holds
1,024 lights. The renderer logs a warning and leaves out the rest.

## Fog is lit by its own light tiles

Height fog gathers light along each view ray, not at the surface a pixel shows,
so the light clusters can't light it: a cluster holds the lights near the
surfaces in it, and leaves out a lamp hanging in the open air in front of them.
The visibility pipeline lights the fog in two compute passes of its own, after
the shadow maps and the opaque depth exist:

1. The tile pass splits the screen into a fixed grid of 32 × 18 tiles and
   records, one bit per light, the lights whose reach overlaps both the tile's
   view from the camera outward and the fog. The fog is the box of each layer's
   dense part: its own box, cut at the height where its density falls under
   0.0001 per meter.
2. The march runs at half resolution. Each pixel visits its tile's lights and
   writes the light they scatter toward the camera into the `Fog Light` target.
   The height fog pass upsamples it with depth-aware weights and adds the sky's
   light.

The march spends its samples on the light alone, because the fog's
transmittance has a closed form. A shadowed sun splits the ray into segments
that each weigh the closed-form transmittance at their start less that at their
end, so unshadowed fog equals the closed form exactly, and only shadows add
error (Hillaire, Physically Based and Unified Volumetric Rendering in Frostbite,
2015). A point or cone light places its samples at equal steps of the angle the
ray subtends at the light, which cancels its inverse-square falloff (Kulla and
Fajardo, Importance Sampling Techniques for Path Tracing in Participating
Media, 2012). Cones also clip the ray to their cone first. One fixed blue-noise
value per pixel offsets every light's samples, so the grain doesn't flicker and
no temporal filter is needed.

A change here must keep these rules:

- Without shadows, a sun's marched light equals the closed form at any sample
  count.
- The march writes zero alpha only where it marched nothing. The height fog
  pass reads zero alpha as no march and falls back to the unshadowed sun, which
  also covers pipelines without a march and frames whose march pipelines are
  still compiling.
- Each layer's density and transmittance at a sample come from one exponential
  of the ray's clipped part of the layer, and a sun's cascades step outward with
  its samples. Both keep the cost per sample flat; measure `stage.fog-light`
  before and after a change.

The visibility tests `fog_light_tiles_hold_the_lights_that_reach_the_fog` and
the `fog_light_of_*` tests guard the culling, the closed form, the shadow
lookup, and the point and cone estimates against reference integrals. The
height fog tests guard the blend.

## The sky's haze uses the delta-Eddington approximation

The atmosphere sky builds three lookup tables: transmittance, multiple
scattering, and the sky view. The multiple-scattering table sums every
scattering order after the first in closed form, which only works if light
leaves each scattering event evenly in all directions. Air nearly does. Haze
doesn't: with the default anisotropy of 0.76 it throws most of its light
forward. Fed to the table unchanged, a clear day's haze spreads that light over
the whole sky and turns it white.

The sky therefore scales the haze with the delta-Eddington approximation
(Joseph, Wiscombe, and Weinman, 1976, *The Delta-Eddington Approximation for
Radiative Flux Transfer*). For a phase function of anisotropy `g`, the fraction
`g²` of the scattered light counts as a forward peak that was never scattered,
and the remaining `1 − g²` scatters with the much lower anisotropy
`g / (1 + g)`. `haze_transport_scale` in `render_passes/sky.rs` returns that
`1 − g²`, and the pass uploads both the full and the scaled haze scattering:

| Where | Haze it uses |
| --- | --- |
| Transmittance table, which dims sunlight and the view | Scaled |
| Multiple-scattering table | Scaled, as an even scatterer |
| Sky view, light the haze scatters once toward the eye | Full, with the Cornette-Shanks phase function |
| Sky view, everything else | Scaled |

The once-scattered term keeps the true phase function because that light is
what draws the glow around the sun, and the scaled haze has no forward peak
left to draw it with. This follows the single-scattering correction of Nakajima
and Tanaka (1988), which adds exact single scattering to a solution computed
with a truncated phase function.

A change to the sky must keep these true:

- Every table reads the scaled haze. A table that reads the full haze whitens
  the sky again.
- Only the once-scattered haze term reads the full haze and the anisotropy.
- The multiple-scattering table still treats the scaled haze as an even
  scatterer, though its anisotropy is `g / (1 + g)`, not zero. That's the
  table's own approximation, not part of delta-Eddington.

`forward_scattering_haze_dims_the_sun_like_thinner_even_haze` and
`haze_glows_around_the_sun` in `render_passes/sky.rs` guard the scaling and the
glow.
