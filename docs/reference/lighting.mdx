Byte Engine lights a scene in physical units, so a light responds predictably
to distance and material properties. This page defines the light shapes, the
units they take and how they convert, and the limits on reach, count, and
shadows. To create lights and set up exposure, environment, and fog, follow
[Light a scene](/docs/use/lighting).

## Light shapes

Byte Engine provides three analytic light shapes. Point and cone lights can also use a baked IES profile when you need a measured angular intensity distribution:

| Light | Best intensity unit | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `DirectionalLight` | Illuminance in [lux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lux) | Sunlight or another source that's effectively infinitely far away |
| `PointLight` | Luminous intensity in [candela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candela) or total flux in [lumens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(unit)) | Bare bulbs and other omnidirectional sources |
| `ConeLight` | Luminous intensity in [candela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candela) or total flux in [lumens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_(unit)) | Spotlights, flashlights, and focused fixtures |

One world-space distance unit is one meter. Keep your scene at that scale so inverse-square
attenuation and distance-based conversions remain meaningful.

## Color

`LightColor::TemperatureKelvin` names a blackbody-style illuminant, which Byte
Engine converts through CIE chromaticity to linear sRGB. A Kelvin value must be
from 1,000 K through 40,000 K. `LightColor::LinearSrgb` names an authored
color. Its RGB values describe chromaticity, not brightness: Byte Engine
normalizes the color to unit photopic luminance, so only `PhotometricIntensity`
sets brightness. RGB components must be nonnegative and the color must have
nonzero luminance.

## Intensity conversions

Every `PhotometricIntensity` variant works with every light shape. Byte Engine
resolves the authored quantity on the CPU into illuminance in lux for a
directional light and luminous intensity in candela for a point or cone light:

| Variant | Fields | Directional light | Point and cone lights |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Illuminance` | `lux`, `measurement_distance_m` | `E = lux` | `I = E × d²`, where `d` is the measurement distance |
| `LuminousIntensity` | `candela`, `reference_distance_m` | `E = I / d²`, where `d` is the reference distance | `I = candela` |
| `LuminousFlux` | `lumens`, `directional_beam_area_m2` | `E = Φ / A`, where `A` is the beam area | Point: `I = Φ / 4π`. Cone: `I = Φ / Ω`, where `Ω = π × (2 − cos(inner) − cos(outer))` |
| `Luminance` | `nits`, `projected_area_m2`, `reference_distance_m` | `E = L × A / d²` | `I = L × A` |

Every field is validated for every shape, even when that shape's conversion
doesn't use it. A cone light's angular falloff is
`clamp((cosine − cos(outer)) / (cos(inner) − cos(outer)), 0, 1)` on both the CPU
and the GPU, and cone angles are half angles measured from the light direction.
Analytic lights don't retain the physical emitter shape after the conversion.

Light constructors return `PhotometricError` when a color, magnitude, distance,
or area is zero, negative, or not finite. They also return it for a cone whose
half angles aren't finite, whose inner angle is negative or not below its outer
angle, or whose outer angle exceeds π; for an IES profile with an empty resource
ID or a dimmer outside 0 through 1; and for a directional light's angular radius
outside 0 up to, but not including, π/2.

## IES profiles

A baked IES profile supplies a point or cone light's peak candela and its
relative intensity in every direction. `LightColor` only tints it, and `dimmer`
scales it linearly from `0.0` for off through `1.0` for the measured output. An
`Orientation` places the profile:

- Local `+Z` is the emission axis.
- Local `+X` is the IES C0 plane.
- Local `+Y` is the IES C90 plane.

## Light reach and limits

A point or cone light reaches as far as its exposed illuminance stays above 1/1024 lux. On a white surface, that
much light shows as about one step of an 8-bit display, so lights keep their inverse-square falloff without a
visible edge. A brighter light, or a brighter camera exposure, reaches farther. Directional lights reach everything.

A scene can hold up to 1,024 lights. If it holds more, the renderer logs a warning and leaves out the lights past
that limit.

## Shadow maps

A point light casts shadows through one cube map and a cone light through one
perspective shadow map. A cone with an outer half angle of 90° or more remains
a valid light but can't use one perspective shadow map.

A local light's shadow reaches from 0.1 m to where its exposure-weighted peak
illuminance falls below 0.125 lux. A light can replace either end of that range.

The visibility pipeline specifies which lights get shadow maps, how large they
are, and how often they're drawn, under
[shadow maps](/docs/reference/rendering#shadow-maps).

## Height fog lighting

An `ExponentialHeightFog` takes light from the sky and from the scene's lights. Each view ray
gathers the light its fog scatters toward the camera, single scattering only:

| Term | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Opacity | 1 − T(d) for a surface d meters along the ray, where T(t) = e^(−τ(t)) and τ(t) is the sum of each layer's density integrated from the camera to t. A layer's density is its base density × e^(−falloff × (height − base height)) inside its box, and 0 outside it. The opacity is held to the fog's maximum opacity. |
| Background | A pixel with no surface has d = 100 km. |
| Sky light | The ambient illuminance E gives every point of fog a radiance of albedo × E / π from every direction, unshadowed. |
| Phase | Henyey-Greenstein with the fog's anisotropy g, at the scattering angle: the angle between the direction the light travels and the direction from the fog toward the camera. Looking straight at a light gives an angle of 0. |
| Light toward the fog | A light reaches a point of fog undimmed by the fog between them. |
| Lights | Every light in the scene's light table, up to its limit. Directional lights light every point of fog. Point and cone lights light fog within their reach, at their peak intensity in every direction: IES profiles don't apply, the inverse-square falloff and a cone's angular falloff do. |
| Fog volume | A point or cone light lights the fog only where its reach overlaps a layer's box or, for a layer without one, lies below the height where the layer's density falls under 0.0001 per meter. |
| Shadows | A light with a shadow map shadows each sample of fog. A directional or cone light's sample blends the depth comparisons of the four texels around its position bilinearly; a point light's sample compares the one texel its direction selects. |
| Directional shadow distance | A directional light's shadow reaches the far end of its last cascade, along the camera's view axis. Fog past it, and fog that no cascade holds, is lit. A sample takes the first cascade, from the one its depth falls in, that holds it. |
| Local shadow range | A cone or point light shadows fog between its shadow's near and far planes. Fog outside that range is lit. |

### Light samples

| Setting | Default | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light samples n | 16 | 1 through 64 per view ray, for each directional light with a shadow map and each point or cone light whose lit part of the fog the ray crosses. |

| Light | Placement |
| --- | --- |
| Directional, unshadowed | No samples: its light is the opacity × phase × albedo × illuminance. |
| Directional, shadowed | Over the shadowed length L, the shorter of d and the shadow distance, sample k of n lies at L × ((k + ξ) / n)², for k from 0. It weighs T at its segment's start less T at its end, the segment running from L × (k / n)² to L × ((k + 1) / n)². Past L, the fog is lit and weighs T(L) − T(d). |
| Point or cone | Over the part of the ray within the light's reach, and for a cone with an outer half angle below 80° within its cone, sample k of n lies at equal steps of the angle the ray subtends at the light, at (k + ξ) / n of the way. |

ξ is the first channel of the engine's 64 × 64 blue noise at the march pixel, modulo 64. It's the
same every frame.

Where nothing shadows a light, the shadowed and unshadowed placements give the same light.

### Resolution

| Rule | Value |
| --- | --- |
| March | Half the sink's resolution. A march pixel takes the nearest of the four sink pixels it covers when its row and column add up to an even number, and the farthest otherwise. |
| Upsample | Each sink pixel blends the four nearest march pixels by their bilinear weight times 1 / (0.01 + 16 × \|log₂(1 + d) − log₂(1 + dᵢ)\|), where dᵢ is a march pixel's distance. |
| Pipelines | The visibility pipeline lights the fog this way. With another scene pipeline, only the sky and the newest directional light light the fog, unshadowed. |

## Sky and calibration

If you install the atmosphere sky with `setup_atmosphere_sky_render_pass`, the newest directional
light is also the sky's sun, whether it was created before or after the window. Its lux and color set how bright and what color the sky is, so a
brighter sun brightens the sky too. Without a directional light, the sky is black. The sky includes
light that scatters more than once in the air, and sunlight the ground reflects back up, so a
100,000 lux sun high in the sky gives, at the default haze and away from the sun, a clear sky of
about 3,400 nits well above the horizon and 6,500 near it, and several times more around the sun.

The same sky is the scene's environment while the scene has no `Environment`: global illumination,
reflections, and the rest of the environment's light take the sky's light in the same units, with
no calibration. See [sky environment](/docs/reference/rendering#sky-environment).

Haze is the aerosol optical depth looking straight up from the ground. It scatters every color
alike and mostly forward, so more haze puts a white glow around the sun and pales the rest of the
sky a little, most near the horizon:

| Haze | Air |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | No aerosols, only air molecules and ozone |
| 0.02 | Very clean air |
| 0.05 | A clear day; the default |
| 0.15 | An average day over land |
| 0.4 | A hazy day |

Haze takes any number of 0 or more. It thins with altitude over the Mie scale height, so its
scattering at the ground is the haze divided by that height. For a Mie anisotropy `g`:

| Light | Haze scattering it meets |
| --- | --- |
| Scattered once by the haze toward the eye | The full haze, with the Cornette-Shanks phase function |
| Sunlight on its way down, the view, and light scattered more than once | The haze × (1 − g²), scattered evenly |

The light's illuminance is the sunlight that reaches the camera, the same light that surfaces
receive. The sunlight that enters the atmosphere is that illuminance divided, for each color
channel, by the atmosphere's transmittance from the camera toward the sun:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Sunlight entering the atmosphere | Illuminance ÷ max(transmittance toward the sun, 1/16), for each color channel |
| Sky radiance | Scattering for each lux entering the atmosphere × the sunlight entering the atmosphere |
| Sun disk radiance | Illuminance ÷ the disk's solid angle, while the transmittance is 1/16 or more; below that, multiplied by 16 × transmittance |
| Sun below the horizon | Transmittance 0, so the sky is 16 times its scattering for each lux and the disk is black |

The sky is drawn after opaque surfaces
and before transparent ones, so glass and other blended surfaces show the sky behind them. By
default the sky covers the upper half of the screen; its `screen_height` setting changes how much.

<Callout type="info" title="Physical calibration limits">
	Emissive materials don't have a unified physical calibration with analytic
	lights. Radiometric units such as watts and watts per square meter aren't
	supported.
</Callout>
