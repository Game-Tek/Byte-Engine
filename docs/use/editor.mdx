Use the editor when you want to arrange a level while you play it. It draws over your application's frame, so the game
keeps running and rendering under it, and every change you make reaches the game at once.

The editor picks what you click with a screen scene query, so it needs the visibility pipeline, which you install with
`setup_pbr_visibility_shading_render_pipeline`. It reads your keys through the default input, which you install with
`setup_default_input`.

## Install the editor

1. Set up your application's own render passes first. The editor's pass must come last, so it draws over everything
   else, your own UI included.

2. Call `install` before the first window opens, with the window's size, and before
   `launch_deferred_tasks_thread`. Render passes register before the first window, and the editor installs the
   pipeline its previews draw with, which the loading thread must start with.

   ```rust
   use byte_engine::ui::Size;

   let mut editor = byte_engine::editor::install(&mut app, Size::new(1920, 1080));
   ```

3. Call `frame` once in every tick, after your game's own tick:

   ```rust
   while app
   	.tick_with(|app, time| {
   		game.tick(app, time);
   		editor.frame(app);
   	})
   	.is_some()
   {}
   ```

The editor starts closed, and a closed editor costs almost nothing: its render pass forwards the frame unchanged.
Its previews draw in a scene of their own, with a visibility pipeline of their own, which holds its GPU memory even
while the editor is closed.

## Edit the scene

1. Press F5 to open the editor. It frees the cursor and takes all of your application's input until you close it
   with F5 or Escape. Escape steps back one thing at a time: it cancels what you're carrying or typing, closes the
   palette, and then the editor. Closing the editor also closes the windows of the graphs and textures it opened.

2. Look around the scene with the editor's own camera. The editor opens on your game's camera; press W, A, S, or D to
   fly forward, left, back, or right, Space to rise, and Shift to sink, and hold the right mouse button and drag to
   turn. The first move switches the view to the editor's camera, which starts where your game's camera stands, so
   your game's camera stays where your game puts it. Closing the editor goes back to your game's camera, and the next
   time it opens, it opens there again.

   The keys move the camera while no field takes text. Click the scene search to type in it, and press Enter or
   Escape, or click elsewhere, to give the keys back to the camera.

3. Click an entity in the game view, or its row in the scene list, to select it. The properties panel shows the
   entity's name, its transform while it has one, and its camera or light settings. A light shows its color and
   intensity in the form it was authored in, such as a temperature in kelvin and a flux in lumens. Its shadow range
   shows `auto` until you set it; type `auto` to hand it back to the renderer.

4. Pick Move, Rotate, or Scale in the toolbar, then drag a handle of the gizmo. To set a value exactly, drag a
   property field sideways, or click it and type the value.

   Press Cmd+Z to undo a move, turn, or scale, and Cmd+Shift+Z to redo it. One drag, from press to release, undoes
   at once, and so does one typed value. An undo changes the entity by what the edit changed it by, so a move undone
   after the game moved the entity too keeps the game's move. See [Editor history](/docs/reference/editor-history)
   for how edits group into steps.

5. Press Cmd+A to open the palette on what you can place: your meshes, your prefabs, and a point, cone, or
   directional light. Type part of a name and drag the result into the scene, or press Enter to carry it with the
   pointer until you click where it goes. A mesh or light follows the pointer; a prefab appears where you place it,
   with its parameters' defaults. A light starts a little above the surface you place it on, a cone or directional
   light facing down; select it to move or turn it.

   Before you type, the palette lists what you used most recently in it first.

   Press Cmd+K instead to search everything, material graphs and settings included. Press the shortcut again to close
   the palette, or another one to switch to it.

   Prefabs spawn through the spawner you give the inspector, so their components can use your own kinds. Give it
   the spawner you register your kinds with, after you register them:

   ```rust
   let mut spawner = Spawner::with_engine_kinds();
   spawner.register::<Patrol>("patrol")?;
   app.inspector().set_spawner(Arc::new(spawner));
   ```

   See [Spawn reusable objects with prefabs](/docs/use/prefabs) to write one.

6. To change a runtime setting, such as `render.lod.bias`, press Cmd+. and type part of its name. Each row shows the
   setting's value. Press Enter on one to put its value in the search field, type a new value, and press Enter again
   to set it. A setting that takes one of a few words, such as `true` or `false` or a technique's name, lists them
   instead: pick one with the arrow keys or a click, or type to narrow the list, and press Enter. Press Escape to go
   back to the search. The values a setting takes are the ones the console's `set` takes; when your application refuses one, a
   toast says why. See [runtime settings](/docs/reference/parameters#runtime-settings) for what each one takes.

7. To explore your resources, press Cmd+E and type part of a name. It finds your material graphs, material variants,
   meshes, and textures. Each opens in a window of its own beside the running game: what you opened fills the
   window, and a drawer on the right holds its name and its settings. Press Escape in the window, or close it, to
   close it. Opening a resource that's already open does nothing.

   Open a mesh to see it lit on a stage of its own. Its drawer on the right shows its size in meters and its totals:
   primitives, materials, vertices, indices, triangles, and detail levels, and whether it has a skeleton. A second
   drawer, on the left, lists its primitives in order. Each shows the material it draws with, its vertex, index,
   triangle, and meshlet counts at full detail, how many [detail levels](/docs/reference/assets#detail-levels) it
   has, and whether it's skinned. On macOS, Option-click a material's name to copy its resource ID, the name your code
   and level files use for it. Primitives that don't fit the drawer's height are counted in its last row. Drag the
   picture to turn the mesh as a trackball: a drag across it turns it the way you drag, and a drag around its edge
   rolls it. Scroll to move closer or farther.

   A mesh with more than one detail level shows a slider at the top of its window. Its first stop, **Auto**, picks
   each primitive's level by distance, as your application does; each stop after it draws that level, from 0, full
   detail, to the coarsest. Drag the knob, or click a stop. The mesh changes level as the knob passes each stop, and
   the knob snaps to the nearest one when you let go.

   Open a texture to see it with its size in pixels in the drawer. Scroll over the picture to zoom in or out.
   Textures show from their PNG or JPEG source files, so a build without sources finds none.

   When you zoom in on a mesh or a texture until it's wider than the room between the drawers, the drawers slide away
   so it can use the whole window, and they come back once you zoom out until it fits again. What you look at stays
   centered in the room between the drawers.

   The drawer of a texture shows the texture's [image settings](/docs/reference/assets#image-settings): its semantic
   and color space, which come from its file name, and the settings you can change: mips, compression, coverage, and
   CPU readable. It shows the values the texture bakes with, from its BEAD sidecar and directory defaults, or the
   defaults for its semantic when nothing sets them. Click a switch, or click the coverage field and type a cutoff
   from 0 to 1 or `off`, and the editor writes the change to the texture's sidecar, such as `rock_albedo.jpg.bead`,
   creating it when it doesn't exist. Your application then bakes the texture again. The editor writes the sidecar
   as plain JSON, so comments in it don't survive a change.

8. To start a new level, press Cmd+K, pick **New level**, and pick the folder it goes in; `/` is the asset folder
   itself. To put it in a folder that doesn't exist yet, type the folder's path, such as `levels/arena`, and pick
   the **New folder** row; the editor creates the folder with the level. The editor writes an empty level there,
   named `untitled.level`, or a numbered name, such as `untitled 2.level`, when that name is taken. The level isn't
   loaded into the running game.

9. To start a new material, press Cmd+K, pick **New material**, and pick its folder as for a level. The editor
   writes a [root material](/docs/reference/bema#material-files) with no parameters, `untitled.bema`, and its graph,
   `untitled.bemx`, which holds only a surface node, and opens the graph so you can build the surface.

   To start a variant, pick **New variant**, then the root material it specializes, then its folder. The editor
   writes a [variant](/docs/reference/bema#variant-files), such as `paint variant.bema`, that gives every parameter
   the material's own value, so it looks like the material until you change one. While a material's graph is open,
   the palette lists **New variant** rows first, one for each root material that uses the graph, so you can skip
   picking the material. The new variant opens to change its parameters.

   To change a variant's parameters, find it with Cmd+E and open it. Its window shows a sphere wearing the
   variant, which you turn and zoom as you do a mesh, and which follows each change once your application bakes it.
   Its drawer lists its parent's parameters and the value the variant gives each, or the parent's default when it
   gives none. Click a value and type a new one, then press Enter: a number for a `float`; three or four numbers,
   apart by commas or spaces, or a color name such as `Purple`, for a `vec3f` or `vec4f`; or an image's asset ID for a
   `Texture2D`. Text that isn't one changes nothing. The editor writes the change to the variant's file, and your
   application bakes the material again. It writes plain JSON, so comments in the file don't survive a change.

10. To change a material, find its graph with Cmd+E or Cmd+K and open it. The graph opens in a window of its own. In
    that window, drag between sockets to connect nodes, press Cmd+A to add one, and press Cmd+S to save the graph; your
    application reloads the material. Press Escape in the window, or close it, when you're done. Undo and redo apply
    to the scene, in the game's window.

## See warnings and errors

While the editor is open, each warning and error your application logs shows as a toast in the window's bottom-right
corner: what happened, over its likely cause. The newest toast is in front, with up to two older ones behind it. Move
the pointer over them to spread them out and keep them from leaving; otherwise each one leaves after four seconds, or
when you click its ×. A message that repeats counts up on its toast instead of adding another.

Toasts show what `log.level` lets through, which is only errors unless you change it. To see warnings too, run with
`RUST_LOG=warn` or set [`log.level`](/docs/reference/parameters) to `warn`.
